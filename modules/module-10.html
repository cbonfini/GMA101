10 Module overview

Explores how migration interacts with global labor markets, remittances, and economic development, including migrant labor exploitation, livelihoods, entrepreneurship, financial inclusion, and informal economies.

Skill emphasis: Applied + Economic

◎ Learning outcomes

Undergraduate

  1. Explain migrant labor contributions to economies
  2. Describe remittances and their impacts
  3. Identify sectors relying on migrant labor and recognize exploitation risks
  4. Discuss challenges faced by migrant workers

Graduate

  1. Analyze global labor migration systems
  2. Design economic inclusion strategies
  3. Evaluate debates on migration, labor rights, and development

✦ Signature learning experience

✎

Active-learning assignment

Livelihood design challenge for a migrant population

View full assignment sheet✎

Purpose

A design challenge: teams develop a realistic livelihood intervention for a specific migrant population in a specific place — grounded in evidence about what actually improves economic outcomes, honest about constraints like work authorization, credential recognition, and exploitation risk.

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Describe the population's current labor market position (UG 1)
  • Identify their economic contributions and constraints (UG 2)
  • Explain the challenges — legal, financial, social — that the design must address (UG 3)
  • Recognize exploitation risks and build safeguards into the design (UG 4)

Graduate extension

Graduate students add an evaluation framework: theory of change, measurable indicators, comparison logic, and a funder-style two-page executive summary — turning the design into a fundable proposal (Grad 1–2).

Assignment steps

  1. In teams, choose a population and place (e.g., resettled refugees in a mid-size US city; Venezuelan professionals in Bogotá facing credential barriers; migrant women in informal domestic work; newcomer youth aging out of school support).
  2. Diagnose the binding constraint: research what most limits this group's livelihoods — not what's most visible, what's most binding.
  3. Review evidence on what works: ILO and World Bank livelihood studies, RCT evidence from IRC/J-PAL, documented program models (credential fast-tracking, microenterprise, worker co-ops, sector-based training).
  4. Design the intervention: who it serves, what it does, who runs it, what it costs roughly, and how it avoids known failure modes (creaming, dependency, displacement of other workers).
  5. Name the exploitation risks in your chosen sector and show the specific safeguards in the design.
  6. Pitch the design to the class in ten minutes; the class plays skeptical funders. Revise once and submit.

Deliverables & format

  • Design document, 2,000–2,500 words per team, with a one-page program logic diagram (graduate adds evaluation framework and executive summary)
  • Evidence appendix: what works, with sources
  • Pitch deck or one-pager used in the presentation

Grading criteria

  • Sharpness of the constraint diagnosis (25%)
  • Evidence base — design choices traced to what works (30%)
  • Realism: costs, operators, failure modes, safeguards (25%)
  • Pitch quality and team response to challenge questions (20%)

⚑ Service learning project

⚑

Working Together: Supporting a Worker Center or Migrant Livelihood Program

Support a worker center, financial-inclusion nonprofit, or immigrant entrepreneurship program, and co-produce a livelihood resource that responds to a need the partner identifies.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Describe migrant labor roles in the local economy (UG 1)
  • Identify economic contributions concretely (UG 2)
  • Explain challenges migrant workers face (UG 3)
  • Recognize exploitation risks and protections (UG 4)

Suggested community partners

  • Worker centers and wage-theft clinics
  • Immigrant entrepreneurship incubators and microloan programs
  • Financial literacy nonprofits serving newcomers
  • Farmworker or domestic worker advocacy organizations

Project steps

  1. Complete partner orientation; learn what the partner sees as the priority need.
  2. Serve directly: workshop logistics, ESL-for-work practice, childcare during trainings, outreach material distribution.
  3. Co-produce one resource the partner requests: know-your-rights one-pager, small-business resource guide, banking-access explainer (partner reviews everything).
  4. Learn the local labor market context: which sectors depend on migrant labor and what risks workers report.
  5. Deliver the resource and gather partner feedback.
  6. Reflection: connect what you saw to module debates on labor, contribution, and exploitation.

Deliverables

  • Co-produced livelihood resource (partner keeps, partner-approved)
  • Service log
  • 3-page reflection on labor and economy themes

Grading criteria

  • Service reliability and partner feedback (30%)
  • Accuracy and usefulness of the resource (30%)
  • Understanding of local labor market dynamics (20%)
  • Reflection quality (20%)

Time commitment: 15 hours. Ethics: Students never collect immigration status or wage information; all outreach materials are partner-approved.

✍ Graduate research project

✍

Designing Economic Inclusion: A Strategy and Evaluation Framework

Design an economic inclusion strategy for a specific migrant population in a specific place — grounded in evidence on what works — and build the evaluation framework that would test it.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Design economic inclusion strategies (Grad 1)
  • Evaluate labor policy impacts (Grad 2)

Sample research questions

  • What binding constraints (credential recognition, work authorization, banking access, discrimination) most limit your chosen population's livelihoods?
  • Which interventions have credible evidence of easing those constraints elsewhere?
  • How would you know your strategy worked — what outcomes, measured how, compared to what?

Methods

Evidence review (ILO, World Bank, IRC/J-PAL livelihood evaluations) plus local labor market analysis from public data. The strategy must include a theory of change and a realistic evaluation design (indicators, comparison logic, timeline).

IRB / research ethics

Not required for the design; required later only if the strategy were field-tested with human subjects.

Deliverables

  • 4,500-word strategy paper with theory-of-change diagram
  • Evaluation framework matrix
  • Funder-style two-page executive summary

Grading criteria

  • Evidence base for chosen interventions (30%)
  • Coherence of the theory of change (25%)
  • Rigor of the evaluation design (30%)
  • Executive summary quality (15%)

▤ Peer-reviewed readings

Show 6 readings6
  • DOIMigration and Development: A Theoretical Perspective
    Hein de Haas · 2010 · International Migration Review, 44(1), 227–264 · 10.1111/j.1747-7379.2009.00804.x

    A foundational review article that critically surveys optimistic and pessimistic theories of migration-development linkages, proposing an integrative 'aspiration-capability' framework. Essential for faculty teaching the contested relationship between remittances, migration, and development outcomes in origin countries.

    Access article →
  • DOITheories of International Migration: A Review and Appraisal
    Douglas S. Massey, J. Arango, Graeme Hugo, A. Kouaouci, Adela Pellegrino, J. E. Taylor · 1993 · Population and Development Review, 19(3), 431–466 · 10.2307/2938462

    The canonical comparative review of migration theories—neoclassical economics, new economics, dual labor market, and world systems theory. Still the standard entry point for teaching the structural economic drivers of migration and its global labor market dimensions.

    Access article →
  • DOISituating Platform Gig Economy in the Formal Subsumption of Reproductive Labor: Transnational Migrant Domestic Workers and the Continuum of Exploitation and Precarity
    Siyuan Yin · 2023 · Capital & Class, 47(1), 35–55 · 10.1177/03098168221145407

    Examines how digital labor platforms extend and intensify exploitation of transnational migrant domestic workers, situating platform capitalism within histories of racialised and gendered labor control. Directly addresses migrant labor exploitation and the intersection of immigration policy with informal economies.

    Access article →
  • DOILabor Migration, Remittances, and the Economy in the Gulf Cooperation Council Region
    Udaya R. Wagle · 2024 · Comparative Migration Studies, 12(1), article 29 · 10.1186/s40878-024-00390-3

    Provides recent empirical analysis of the complex and sometimes negative macro-level relationship between mass labor migration and economic growth in the Gulf, a major destination region. Useful for complicating simplistic narratives about remittances as unambiguous development tools.

    Access article →
  • DOIPlatform Work in the Domestic and Home Care Sector: New Mechanisms of Invisibility and Exploitation of Women Migrant Workers
    Paula Rodríguez-Modroño, Astrid Agenjo-Calderón, Purificación López-Igual · 2022 · Work, Employment and Society, 37(4), 983–999 · 10.1080/13552074.2022.2121060

    Applies feminist political economy to show how care platforms exploit intersecting inequalities of gender, race, and immigration status among migrant women workers in the Global South. Connects migrant labor exploitation to care chains, precarious livelihoods, and platform capitalism.

    Access article →
  • DOIDigital Financial Inclusion and Remittances: An Empirical Study on Bangladeshi Migrant Households
    Kazi Abdul Mannan, Khandaker Mursheda Farhana · 2023 · FinTech, 2(4), 556–570 · 10.3390/fintech2040038

    Empirical study demonstrating how migrant remittances drive adoption of digital financial services in Bangladesh, providing evidence on financial inclusion mechanisms. Relevant for teaching financial inclusion, remittance flows, and the economic impact on origin-country households.

    Access article →

▷ Video resources

Show 5 videos5
YouTube The Hidden Force in Global Economics: Sending Money Home
TED / Dilip Ratha (World Bank) · 2014 · 17:04

World Bank economist Dilip Ratha explains how the $413 billion migrants sent home in 2013 dwarfs global foreign aid, making remittances a critical lever for development. Faculty can use this to open discussions on the macroeconomic role of migrant labor and the hidden architecture of global financial flows.

YouTube The Economics of Remittances: Is Migrant Work Worth It? | In Conversation | Dilip Ratha, World Bank
CNA Insider · 2022 · 22:29

An in-depth interview with Dilip Ratha (World Bank) that examines whether migrant work is economically rational for workers and sending countries, covering remittance fees, pandemic impacts, and development trade-offs. Useful for case-study analysis of the costs and benefits of labor migration.

YouTube The Brutal Economics of Migrant Labour
Economics Explained · 2022 · 13:12

A data-driven explainer covering why migrants move for work, the global labor shortage, remittance flows, and negative impacts on both sending and receiving economies. Good for introducing students to the structural economic forces driving labor migration.

YouTube Human Trafficking Is All Around You. This Is How It Works
TED / Noy Thrupkaew · 2015 · 18:52

Journalist Noy Thrupkaew exposes how forced labor and human trafficking are embedded in global supply chains that produce everyday goods. Faculty can use this TED talk to shift discussions from remittances to the darker side of migrant labor, including exploitation and trafficking.

YouTube How Migrant Workers Are Vital to the U.S. Economy
CBS News · 2023 · 4:57

A concise news segment illustrating U.S. dependence on migrant workers across agriculture, construction, and services, set against recent tragedies involving migrant workers. Useful as a brief current-events entry point before deeper discussion of labor rights and exploitation.
