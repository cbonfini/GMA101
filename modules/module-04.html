4 Module overview

Explores the social, economic, political, and environmental drivers that influence migration decisions, emphasizing multi-causality and the complexity of push and pull factors.

Skill emphasis: Analytical + Systems Thinking

◎ Learning outcomes

Undergraduate

  1. Identify push and pull factors
  2. Explain how economic, political, and environmental conditions influence migration
  3. Describe how drivers interact and recognize multi-causality
  4. Apply drivers to real migration examples

Graduate

  1. Analyze structural factors shaping migration drivers
  2. Model complex driver interactions
  3. Evaluate competing and dominant explanations for migration decisions

✦ Signature learning experience

✎

Active-learning assignment

Case mapping: layered drivers in one migration flow

View full assignment sheet✎

Purpose

Single-cause stories dominate public debate about why people migrate. In this case-mapping exercise, students take one real migration flow and build a layered map showing how economic, political, environmental, and violence-related drivers interact — replacing 'push/pull' lists with systems thinking.

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Identify the key drivers present in one real flow (UG 1)
  • Explain push/pull complexity beyond a two-column list (UG 2)
  • Analyze a case with credible evidence for each claimed driver (UG 3)
  • Recognize multi-causality and interaction effects (UG 4)

Graduate extension

Graduate students formalize the map into a causal loop diagram with every link evidenced, and write an evaluation of the dominant public narrative about their chosen flow against the mapped evidence (Grad 1–2).

Assignment steps

  1. Choose one flow with instructor approval (e.g., Central America–US, Venezuela–Colombia, West Africa–Canary Islands, Myanmar–Bangladesh, climate-affected internal displacement in the Sahel).
  2. Research the drivers using at least six credible sources: academic literature, UNHCR/IOM reporting, World Bank data, quality country reporting.
  3. Draft the layered map: drivers grouped by type, with arrows showing how they interact (e.g., drought → crop failure → rural unemployment → gang recruitment pressure → flight). Every arrow needs a source.
  4. Annotate the map: which drivers are proximate vs. structural? Which would remain if the most-cited driver disappeared?
  5. Write the accompanying analysis explaining the map and what it reveals that the common narrative misses.
  6. Present the map in a five-minute gallery walk; revise once based on peer and instructor feedback.

Deliverables & format

  • One-page visual driver map (hand-drawn, slides, or diagram tool — clarity matters, polish doesn't)
  • Written analysis, 1,200–1,500 words (graduate: 2,000+ with causal loop diagram and narrative evaluation)
  • Source list with a one-line note on what each source evidenced

Grading criteria

  • Evidence quality — every mapped link supported (30%)
  • Sophistication of interaction analysis, not just driver listing (30%)
  • Clarity and honesty of the visual map (20%)
  • Written analysis and presentation (20%)

⚑ Service learning project

⚑

Layered Drivers in Our Community: Country-Conditions Research for Casework

Support a legal aid or immigrant services organization by producing supervised country-conditions or driver-mapping summaries that help staff understand why their clients migrated.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Identify key drivers in real cases (UG 1)
  • Explain push/pull complexity beyond single-cause stories (UG 2)
  • Analyze case examples (UG 3)
  • Recognize multi-causality (UG 4)

Suggested community partners

  • Immigration legal aid organizations
  • Refugee resettlement caseworker teams
  • Migrant rights advocacy groups needing background research
  • Public defender or pro-bono asylum support projects

Project steps

  1. Meet the partner to select 1–2 origin countries or corridors relevant to their caseload.
  2. Research economic, political, environmental, and violence-related drivers using credible sources (UNHCR, IOM, World Bank, academic literature, country reports).
  3. Build a layered driver map showing how drivers interact (not a simple push/pull list).
  4. Have the partner review for accuracy and usefulness; revise.
  5. Deliver the summaries in the partner's preferred format and walk staff through them.
  6. Write a reflection on how the research changed your understanding of 'why people migrate.'

Deliverables

  • Country-conditions/driver summary (partner keeps)
  • Layered driver interaction map (visual)
  • 2-page reflection on multi-causality

Grading criteria

  • Source quality and accuracy (30%)
  • Sophistication of the interaction analysis — beyond push/pull lists (30%)
  • Partner usefulness, confirmed by feedback (25%)
  • Reflection quality (15%)

Time commitment: 15 hours. Ethics: Students work from public sources only and never access client files or case details.

✍ Graduate research project

✍

Modeling Multi-Causality: A Systems Analysis of One Migration Flow

Build a systems model (causal loop diagram or equivalent) of the interacting drivers behind one significant migration flow, and use it to evaluate the dominant public narrative about that flow.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Model complex driver interactions (Grad 1)
  • Evaluate dominant narratives against evidence (Grad 2)

Sample research questions

  • How do economic, political, environmental, and violence drivers reinforce or dampen each other in your chosen flow?
  • Where does the dominant narrative ('they come for jobs' / 'it's all violence') diverge from the evidence?
  • Which intervention points does the systems model suggest, and which popular interventions does it predict will fail?

Methods

Case-study method combining published data and scholarship with formal systems mapping (causal loop diagram; optionally a simple stock-and-flow model). Justify every causal link with a cited source.

IRB / research ethics

Not required — published sources only.

Deliverables

  • 4,000-word case analysis with the systems model as a central figure
  • Annotated model appendix documenting evidence for each link

Grading criteria

  • Rigor of the systems model — every link evidenced (35%)
  • Quality of the narrative evaluation (25%)
  • Case knowledge and source quality (25%)
  • Clarity of writing and visuals (15%)

▤ Peer-reviewed readings

Show 6 readings6
  • DOIDrivers of Migration: Why Do People Move?
    Castelli, Francesco · 2018 · Journal of Travel Medicine, 25(1), tay040 · 10.1093/jtm/tay040

    An accessible, peer-reviewed synthesis of the main drivers of migration across economic, political, environmental, and conflict dimensions, explicitly covering push-pull dynamics and multi-causality. Well-suited as an introductory reading for students new to the field due to its clarity and breadth.

    Access article →
  • DOIDeciphering Interwoven Drivers of Environment-Related Migration: A Multisite Case Study from the Ethiopian Highlands
    Groth, Johanna; Ide, Tobias; Sakdapolrak, Patrick; Kassa, Endeshaw; & Hermans, Kathleen · 2020 · Global Environmental Change, 63, 102094 · 10.1016/j.gloenvcha.2020.102094

    Using Qualitative Comparative Analysis in Ethiopia, this study shows that capabilities and migrant networks—not environmental push factors alone—drive environment-related migration. Directly demonstrates the aspirations-capabilities framework in an empirical setting and underscores multi-causality over simplistic environmental determinism.

    Access article →
  • DOICOVID-19 Responses Restricted Abilities and Aspirations for Mobility and Migration: Insights from Diverse Cities in Four Continents
    Jolivet, Delphine; Fransen, Sonja; Adger, W. Neil; et al. · 2023 · Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 10(1), 191 · 10.1057/s41599-023-01721-y

    Drawing on in-depth qualitative data from six cities across four continents, this study applies Carling's aspiration/ability model to COVID-19 disruptions of migration. It vividly shows how macro shocks simultaneously reshape both aspirations and capabilities to migrate, making it ideal for teaching migration decision-making frameworks.

    Access article →
  • DOIThe Perils of Seeing Twenty-First Century Migration Through a Twentieth-Century Lens
    Massey, Douglas S. · 2018 · International Social Science Journal, 68(227–228), 51–61 · 10.1111/ISSJ.12173

    Massey argues that conventional theories fail to account for twenty-first-century drivers including climate change, forced displacement, and South–South flows. An authoritative critique that pushes students beyond classic economic models toward understanding the contemporary complexity of migration drivers.

    Access article →
  • DOIHumanising Research on Migration Decision-Making: A Situated Framework
    Fresnoza-Flot, Asuncion · 2024 · Open Research Europe, 3(142), v2 · 10.12688/openreseurope.16483.2

    Proposes a 'situated' framework that centers human agency and emotional dimensions in migration decision-making, challenging mainstream theories that overemphasize structural factors. Useful for introducing the micro-level aspiration side of the aspirations-capabilities framework and for raising questions about migrant agency.

    Access article →
  • DOIInvoluntary Immobility and Well-Being: A Scoping Review of Existing Literature and Future Research Directions
    Vanden Hautte, Lise; Scheerens, Charlotte; Ruyssen, Ilse; & Toma, Sorana · 2025 · Globalization and Health, 21(1), 57 · 10.1186/s12992-025-01116-5

    Scoping review of 13 studies on the mental health and well-being impacts of involuntary immobility—the condition of wanting but being unable to migrate. Bridges the Module 1 theoretical concept of involuntary immobility with Module 4's discussion of what prevents migration and with Module 8's mental health themes.

    Access article →
