8 Module overview

Focuses on migration routes, transit experiences, and the risks migrants encounter during journeys, including trafficking, exploitation, trauma, gendered and child-specific vulnerabilities, and resilience.

Skill emphasis: Reflective + Applied

◎ Learning outcomes

Undergraduate

  1. Identify major global migration routes and stages of the journey
  2. Describe challenges and risks during migration journeys
  3. Explain how geography and policy shape routes and emotional impacts
  4. Recognize vulnerable groups and analyze case studies of journeys

Graduate

  1. Evaluate geopolitical factors and border regimes shaping routes
  2. Evaluate trauma-informed frameworks
  3. Apply ethical engagement principles to lived-experience material

✦ Signature learning experience

✎

Active-learning assignment

Narrative or interview-based analysis (ethics required)

View full assignment sheet✎

Purpose

This module's material is human, not abstract, so its assignment centers real journeys — analyzed ethically. Students work with published first-person narratives (memoirs, oral history archives, documentary testimony) or, with training and consent protocols, an original interview, and analyze one journey through the module's frameworks: stages, risks, trauma, resilience.

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Describe the stages of migration visible in a real narrative (UG 1)
  • Identify risks — trafficking, exploitation, detention — as they actually appeared (UG 2)
  • Explain emotional and psychological impacts using module frameworks, not amateur diagnosis (UG 3)
  • Recognize how gender, age, and other factors shaped the journey (UG 4)

Graduate extension

Graduate students conducting original interviews follow the full ethics protocol (consent, right to withdraw, no trauma solicitation) and analyze their material against trauma-informed frameworks from the literature, noting where frameworks fit poorly (Grad 1–2). Interviews require completed ethics training; instructor confirms whether IRB review applies.

Assignment steps

  1. Complete the mandatory ethics briefing before starting: consent, dignity, no re-traumatization, the difference between analyzing a narrative and mining it.
  2. Choose your source: a published memoir or long-form testimony, a curated oral history archive (e.g., UNHCR storytelling projects, StoryCorps, university archives) — or, for approved students, an original interview with someone who has consented in writing.
  3. Map the journey against the module's stage framework: decision, departure, transit, arrival, adjustment — noting where the real journey defies the tidy model.
  4. Analyze two dimensions in depth (e.g., gendered risk and resilience strategies; child vulnerability and family separation; trauma and post-arrival mental health).
  5. Write the analysis, quoting the narrative sparingly and respectfully — the person is the expert on their experience; you are analyzing structures, not judging choices.
  6. Include a 300-word ethics reflection: what you chose not to use, and why.

Deliverables & format

  • Narrative analysis, 1,500–1,800 words (graduate: 2,500 with framework critique)
  • Journey map (visual or structured outline)
  • Ethics reflection (300 words); signed consent form if an original interview was conducted

Grading criteria

  • Ethical handling of the material — demonstrated, not asserted (25%)
  • Accurate use of stage, risk, and trauma frameworks (30%)
  • Depth of the two-dimension analysis (25%)
  • Respectful, precise writing (20%)

⚑ Service learning project

⚑

Accompaniment: Trauma-Informed Support for Newcomer Programs

After trauma-informed practice training, serve in accompaniment and welcome roles with an organization supporting people who have recently completed difficult migration journeys.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Describe migration stages through service context (UG 1)
  • Identify risks migrants face en route and on arrival (UG 2)
  • Explain emotional impacts without eliciting them (UG 3)
  • Recognize vulnerable groups and appropriate support (UG 4)

Suggested community partners

  • Organizations serving recently arrived families (ALAS-model programs)
  • Newcomer youth programs (art, recreation, homework help)
  • Hospital or clinic navigation programs for new arrivals
  • Shelter or transitional housing welcome programs

Project steps

  1. Complete mandatory trauma-informed engagement training before any community contact (provided in-course or by partner).
  2. Serve in low-pressure, presence-based roles: children's activities, welcome kits, transportation help, recreation programs.
  3. Keep a reflective practice journal focused on your own responses and skills — never on eliciting others' stories.
  4. Build a 'journey-stage service map' of local organizations serving people at arrival, adjustment, and integration stages.
  5. Debrief regularly with the site supervisor; practice boundaries and self-care.
  6. Final reflection: how trauma-informed principles change what 'helping' means.

Deliverables

  • Reflective practice journal (5+ entries)
  • Journey-stage service map (partner keeps)
  • 3-page final reflection on trauma-informed practice

Grading criteria

  • Demonstrated trauma-informed conduct, per supervisor feedback (35%)
  • Consistency and reliability of service (25%)
  • Quality of the service map (20%)
  • Reflection depth (20%)

Time commitment: 16–18 hours plus training. Ethics: Students never solicit migration or trauma narratives. If someone chooses to share, students listen respectfully and follow the partner's response protocol. Mandatory training precedes all contact.

✍ Graduate research project

✍

Caring for the Journey: How Providers Use Trauma-Informed Frameworks

A qualitative study interviewing service providers (not survivors) about how trauma-informed care frameworks guide — and where they fail — their work with people who have experienced difficult migration journeys.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Evaluate trauma-informed frameworks (Grad 1)
  • Apply ethical engagement principles in research design (Grad 2)

Sample research questions

  • How do frontline providers translate trauma-informed principles into daily practice with newcomers?
  • Where do standard frameworks fit poorly — cultural assumptions, gendered experiences, children's needs?
  • What adaptations have practitioners developed that the literature hasn't captured?

Methods

Semi-structured interviews with 4–6 providers (caseworkers, clinicians, program staff), thematically coded, analyzed against published trauma-informed care frameworks (e.g., SAMHSA principles) and the refugee mental health literature. Interviewing providers rather than survivors is a deliberate ethical design choice.

IRB / research ethics

REQUIRED. Submit before any recruitment; provider interviews typically qualify for expedited review. Include consent forms and de-identification plan.

Deliverables

  • IRB protocol (graded milestone)
  • 4,500-word qualitative analysis with coded themes
  • Practitioner-facing summary of findings (1–2 pages)

Grading criteria

  • Ethical rigor of the design and IRB protocol (25%)
  • Interview and coding quality (30%)
  • Critical evaluation of frameworks against field reality (30%)
  • Practitioner summary usefulness (15%)

▤ Peer-reviewed readings

Show 6 readings6
  • DOITorture, Trauma and Posttraumatic Symptoms in Syrian Women Asylum Seekers in the Greek Border Camp of Idomeni
    Guarch-Rubio, Marta; Manzanero, Antonio L.; Roy Delgado, Francisco; Minescu, Anca; & Mohamed, D. · 2025 · Torture Journal, 35(1), 1–17 · 10.7146/torture.v35i1.140862

    Documents high rates of PTSD (78–91%) among Syrian and Kurdish women blocked at the Idomeni camp, linking trauma to both pre-migration violence and conditions in transit camps. Provides concrete evidence for the gendered and cumulative nature of journey-related trauma and the concept of 'torturing environments'.

    Access article →
  • DOI'I Feel Like I Am Less Than Other People': Health-Related Vulnerabilities of Male Migrants Travelling Alone on Their Journey to Europe
    Arsenijević, Jelena; Burtscher, Dejan; Ponthieu, Annick; et al. · 2018 · Social Science & Medicine, 209, 86–94 · 10.1016/j.socscimed.2018.05.038

    Qualitative study with male migrants along the Balkan route reveals how deterrence measures (pushbacks, detention) amplify psychological distress and create 'traditionally invisible' vulnerability. Challenges gender assumptions in humanitarian response and demonstrates how border enforcement policies deepen journey-related harm.

    Access article →
  • DOIBeyond the Border: Clandestine Migration Journeys
    Mainwaring, Ċetta & Brigden, Noelle K. · 2016 · Geopolitics, 21(2), 243–262 · 10.1080/14650045.2016.1165575

    Conceptualizes clandestine migration journeys as a series of cumulatively dangerous passages shaped by geography, state enforcement, and smuggling networks. A key theoretical framework for teaching routes, transit, and the spatial dimensions of irregularized migration that bridge smuggling, risk, and resilience.

    Access article →
  • DOIBad Deals and Tragic Pacts: The Impact of the EU's Management of Migration Through the Externalisation of its Borders on 'Women on the Move'
    Vella, Mary Grace · 2024 · Open Research Europe, 4(188), 1 · 10.12688/openreseurope.18388.1

    Critically examines how the EU–Turkey Deal and subsequent agreements expose women and girls to heightened risks through securitized border management. Directly addresses gender-specific vulnerabilities in migration journeys, border externalization, and the protection gaps created by containment-focused policies.

    Access article →
  • DOIFrom Forced Migration to Forced Arrival: The Campization of Refugee Accommodation in European Cities
    Kreichauf, René · 2018 · Comparative Migration Studies, 6(1), 7 · 10.1186/s40878-017-0069-8

    Introduces the concept of 'campization'—the extension of camp logics into urban arrival spaces—analyzing how containment, exclusion, and temporality are reproduced in cities. Bridges the journey–destination gap and is essential for teaching how route-end conditions continue to shape migrant experiences and resilience.

    Access article →
  • DOIMigrant Deaths in California's Borderlands, 2018–2023
    LaFleur, Marni; On, Kevin; Ceja, Ligia; & Martínez, Daniel E. · 2024 · Journal on Migration and Human Security, 12(4), 234–259 · 10.1177/23315024241291825

    Documents 314 migrant deaths in southern California over five years, linking spikes in mortality to border wall construction and COVID-era deterrence policies. Provides stark empirical data on the lethal consequences of border enforcement, making it highly impactful for teaching about route risks and the ethics of deterrence.

DW's investigative documentary examines how EU migration policy outsources border enforcement to North Africa, funneling migrants onto deadlier routes and enabling exploitation by smugglers. Provides systemic analysis of how policy shapes journey risk — essential for the Migration Journey and Experience module.

Al Jazeera's Fault Lines follows migrants crossing the Darien Gap — one of the world's most deadly migration routes — providing ground-level insight into the dangers, motivations, and demographics of the US-bound migration corridor. Ideal for humanizing the abstract concept of 'migration routes.'

Vox's explainer on migrant deaths in the Mediterranean documents the scale of mortality on Europe-bound sea routes and contextualizes European border enforcement. A concise, data-driven piece appropriate for introducing the concept of border lethality.
