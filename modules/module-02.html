2 Module overview

Examines major global migration patterns and introduces students to migration statistics and data sources. Surfaces gaps in data (children, undocumented, disability) and the political use of migration numbers.

Skill emphasis: Analytical

◎ Learning outcomes

Undergraduate

  1. Identify major migration trends and corridors
  2. Interpret migration statistics and datasets
  3. Describe key migration datasets and recognize missing populations
  4. Recognize common misconceptions about migration numbers

Graduate

  1. Evaluate strengths and limitations of migration datasets
  2. Critique data reliability and bias and propose improved approaches
  3. Assess how migration data shapes policy and public narratives

✦ Signature learning experience

✎

Active-learning assignment

Data brief: identify trends + blind spots

View full assignment sheet✎

Purpose

Students work like analysts: using real datasets, they produce a short professional data brief that identifies genuine migration trends and — just as important — names who is missing from the numbers. The brief format teaches evidence communication for policy audiences.

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Interpret migration datasets from at least two sources (UG 1)
  • Identify trends supported by the data, not headlines (UG 2)
  • Recognize missing populations — children, undocumented people, people with disabilities (UG 3)
  • Explain data limitations in plain language (UG 4)

Graduate extension

Graduate students critique the reliability and bias of their chosen datasets and close the brief with a concrete, feasible proposal for improving data collection on one invisible population (Grad 1–2).

Assignment steps

  1. Choose a region, corridor, or population (e.g., Venezuelan displacement, Mediterranean crossings, South–South labor migration in Asia).
  2. Pull data from at least two of: UN DESA international migrant stock, UNHCR Refugee Data Finder, IOM World Migration Report / Missing Migrants, World Bank remittances.
  3. Identify 2–3 defensible trends; build one chart or table per trend with sources and years labeled.
  4. Write the 'blind spots' section: who is not counted here, why, and how that gap could distort policy conclusions.
  5. Draft the brief in professional format: headline findings up top, evidence in the middle, limitations clearly stated — no burying caveats.
  6. Peer-exchange drafts in class; check each other's charts against the underlying numbers before final submission.

Deliverables & format

  • Data brief, 3–4 pages including 2–3 original charts or tables (graduate adds a 1-page data-improvement proposal)
  • All data sources cited with dataset name, publisher, and year
  • One-paragraph executive summary written for a non-specialist reader

Grading criteria

  • Accuracy of data handling and sourcing (30%)
  • Validity of identified trends — claims match the evidence (25%)
  • Quality of the blind-spots analysis (25%)
  • Professional communication: clarity, charts, executive summary (20%)

⚑ Service learning project

⚑

Local Migration Data Profile for a Community Partner

Build a plain-language migration data profile of your city or county for an organization that serves newcomers — and document who is missing from the official numbers.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Interpret migration datasets (UG 1)
  • Identify trends relevant to the local community (UG 2)
  • Recognize missing populations (UG 3)
  • Explain data limitations to a non-technical audience (UG 4)

Suggested community partners

  • Refugee resettlement agencies needing service-planning data
  • School district ESL/newcomer program offices
  • City or county offices of new Americans / immigrant affairs
  • Food banks or health clinics serving immigrant neighborhoods

Project steps

  1. Meet the partner to learn what data questions matter to their work (e.g., languages spoken, arrivals by year, school enrollment).
  2. Gather data from public sources: American Community Survey, refugee arrival data, school and health department statistics, UN/IOM country profiles.
  3. Interview partner staff about who they serve whom the data does not capture (undocumented residents, children, people with disabilities).
  4. Draft the profile in plain language with 3–4 clear charts; include a 'who is missing from these numbers' section.
  5. Review the draft with the partner for accuracy and usefulness; revise.
  6. Present the final profile to partner staff.

Deliverables

  • 4–6 page community data profile (partner keeps it)
  • 'Data gaps' memo explaining limitations and missing populations
  • Reflection (2 pages) on the politics of counting

Grading criteria

  • Accuracy of data work and sourcing (30%)
  • Honesty and clarity about data limitations (25%)
  • Usefulness to the partner, confirmed by partner feedback (25%)
  • Communication quality — plain language and charts (20%)

Time commitment: 15 hours (partner meetings, data work, presentation). Ethics: Use only aggregate public data; never collect or publish individual-level information about the partner's clients.

✍ Graduate research project

✍

Auditing the Numbers: A Critical Evaluation of a Global Migration Dataset

Select one major migration dataset (UN DESA international migrant stock, IOM Missing Migrants, UNHCR refugee statistics, or World Bank remittances) and audit it: how the data are produced, what they systematically miss, and how they are used politically.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Critique data reliability and bias (Grad 1)
  • Propose improved data approaches (Grad 2)

Sample research questions

  • What definitional and collection choices shape the dataset, and which populations do they render invisible?
  • How has this dataset been used (or misused) in policy or media debates?
  • What concrete, feasible changes would most improve its coverage of children, undocumented people, or people with disabilities?

Methods

Documentary analysis of the dataset's methodology papers plus a reproducible descriptive analysis (spreadsheet or notebook) demonstrating at least one blind spot empirically — e.g., comparing two sources that should agree but do not.

IRB / research ethics

Not required — published aggregate data only.

Deliverables

  • 3,500–4,500-word data audit with the reproducible analysis appended
  • One-page 'improved data approach' proposal written for a policy audience

Grading criteria

  • Technical understanding of how the data are produced (25%)
  • Quality of the empirical demonstration (30%)
  • Insight of the bias/politics critique (25%)
  • Feasibility and specificity of the improvement proposal (20%)

  • DOIAnatomy of a Misfit: International Migration Statistics
    Ahmad-yar, Ahmad Wali & Bircan, Tuba · 2021 · Sustainability, 13(7), 4032 · 10.3390/SU13074032

    A systematic review categorizing the gaps in international migration statistics across UN DESA, IOM, OECD, Eurostat, and UNHCR. Directly useful for teaching what data sources exist, what they measure well or poorly, and the five categories of gap: definitions, drivers, geography, demographics, and time lags.

    Access article →
  • DOIMeasuring Global Migration Flows Using Online Data
    Johnston, Drew; Giraudy, Eugenia; Chi, Guanghua; Abel, Guy J.; & Bailey, Mike · 2025 · Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122(16), e2409418122 · 10.1073/pnas.2409418122

    Uses privacy-protected Facebook data from three billion users to estimate monthly country-to-country migration flows for 181 countries, closely matching official statistics. Excellent for teaching data innovation, the promises and limitations of big data in migration research, and the UN call for improved data collection.

    Access article →
  • DOIInternational Migration Review at 60: Evolving and Emerging Models of International Migration Research
    Menjívar, Cecilia; Kraly, Ellen; & Reed, Holly E. · 2024 · International Migration Review, 58(4), 1–28 · 10.1177/01979183241274751

    Reviews 60 years of migration scholarship, identifying seven broad trends including mixed migration, climate migration, and South-South flows. Ideal for orienting faculty and students to the evolving landscape of migration research and how data priorities have shifted over time.

    Access article →
  • DOIThe Globalization of International Migration? A Conceptual and Data-Driven Synthesis
    Ha, Jiyoung; Dewaard, Jack; Abel, Guy J.; et al. · 2024 · Population and Development Review, 50(4), 1089–1117 · 10.1111/padr.12686

    Uses country-to-country flow estimates from 1990–2020 to examine whether migration is truly 'globalizing', distinguishing intensity from connectivity. Faculty can use this to challenge students to engage critically with commonly cited claims about the growth of global migration.

    Access article →
  • DOIEstimating Global Bilateral Refugee Migration Flows from 1990 to 2015
    Schellerer, Stefan · 2023 · International Migration Review, 57(3), 1207–1234 · 10.1177/01979183231187621

    Generates the first systematic bilateral refugee flow estimates at global scale, revealing a post-1990s decline and a geographic focus shift from Africa to Asia. Demonstrates methodological innovation for filling data gaps in forced-migration statistics and illustrates the difference between stock and flow data.

    Access article →
  • DOIMisinformation and Its Impact on Contested Policy Issues: The Example of Migration Discourses
    Erokhin, Denis; Komendantova, Nadejda; & Albano, Teresa · 2023 · Societies, 13(7), 168 · 10.3390/soc13070168

    Analyzes how misinformation about migration spreads through social media and shapes public attitudes, drawing on 45,000 tweets. Essential for the 'political use of statistics' theme and for discussing why accurate data communication matters in migration policy debates.

    Access article →
