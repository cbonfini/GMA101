9 Module overview

Examines how governments regulate migration through borders, immigration systems, and legal frameworks, including detention, deportation, and the tension between domestic and international law.

Skill emphasis: Legal + Analytical

◎ Learning outcomes

Undergraduate

  1. Explain the role of borders in regulating migration
  2. Identify legal frameworks and immigration policy tools
  3. Describe enforcement practices and rights issues
  4. Compare migration policies across countries

Graduate

  1. Critically analyze migration governance systems
  2. Evaluate legal tensions and policy approaches to border management
  3. Interpret real-world migration law cases

✦ Signature learning experience

✎

Active-learning assignment

Legal analysis: migration law case study

View full assignment sheet✎

Purpose

Students do real legal analysis: one migration case study examined through the frameworks that govern borders — sovereignty, the 1951 Convention, non-refoulement, due process. The assignment teaches how to read law against practice and write a structured legal memo.

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Explain border and enforcement concepts in the chosen case (UG 1)
  • Identify the legal frameworks that apply — domestic and international (UG 2)
  • Describe enforcement practices and the rights issues they raise (UG 3)
  • Understand how sovereignty and rights claims collide in practice (UG 4)

Graduate extension

Graduate students analyze a line of 3–4 related cases rather than one, map the doctrine's trajectory, and defend a position on how the tension should be resolved — then critique their own argument (Grad 1–2).

Assignment steps

  1. Select a case or controversy from the provided list (e.g., a non-refoulement challenge to a border expulsion policy; a family detention case; an externalization agreement litigated in domestic courts; a state–federal enforcement conflict) or propose one.
  2. Establish the facts precisely from primary documents — the ruling itself, filings, or official summaries — not just news coverage.
  3. Identify every legal framework in play: constitutional provisions, statutes, the 1951 Convention and Protocol, regional human rights instruments.
  4. Analyze the core tension: what does the state claim sovereignty permits, what do rights instruments require, and how did the decision-maker resolve it?
  5. Assess the reasoning: is it consistent with precedent and treaty obligations? Where is it vulnerable?
  6. Write it as a legal memo: facts, issues, applicable law, analysis, conclusion. Plain English; every legal claim cited.

Deliverables & format

  • Legal analysis memo, 1,500–2,000 words (graduate: 2,500+, multi-case doctrinal analysis)
  • Framework table listing each legal source and what it requires
  • Case documents list (primary sources used)

Grading criteria

  • Accuracy of legal frameworks and case facts (30%)
  • Quality of the sovereignty-vs-rights analysis (30%)
  • Critical assessment of the reasoning, not just description (25%)
  • Memo structure, clarity, and citation (15%)

⚑ Service learning project

⚑

Watching the Law Work: Immigration Court Observation and Legal Aid Support

Participate in an immigration court observation program or support a legal aid office in non-legal roles, then analyze how legal frameworks from the module operate in practice.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Explain border and enforcement concepts from observation (UG 1)
  • Identify legal frameworks in live proceedings (UG 2)
  • Describe enforcement and adjudication practices (UG 3)
  • Understand rights issues facing respondents (UG 4)

Suggested community partners

  • Immigration court watch / observation programs
  • Immigration legal aid offices (file organization, interpretation logistics, reception)
  • Detention visitation or hotline programs (where available and appropriate)
  • Law school immigration clinics needing undergraduate support

Project steps

  1. Complete the partner's observer or volunteer training, including courtroom conduct rules.
  2. Observe 2–3 immigration court sessions (or serve 4+ shifts in the legal aid role).
  3. Record structured observations: hearing type, representation status, interpreter access, outcomes announced (no names).
  4. Connect observations to module frameworks: due process, non-refoulement, detention standards, discretion.
  5. Interview one attorney or accredited representative about the gap between law on paper and law in the room.
  6. Write the observation report and share aggregate observations with the partner program.

Deliverables

  • Structured observation log
  • 5-page legal observation report linking practice to frameworks
  • Aggregate summary for the court watch program

Grading criteria

  • Observation quality and professionalism (25%)
  • Accurate use of legal concepts (30%)
  • Analysis of the law-in-books vs. law-in-action gap (30%)
  • Contribution to the partner program (15%)

Time commitment: 14–16 hours. Ethics: No recording, no client names, no legal advice; observers follow all court and program rules.

✍ Graduate research project

✍

The Sovereignty–Rights Collision: A Migration Case Law Analysis

A legal research paper analyzing a line of cases on one doctrinal question — non-refoulement, detention, externalization, or expedited removal — across domestic and international law.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Evaluate legal tensions between sovereignty and rights (Grad 1)
  • Interpret real-world cases (Grad 2)

Sample research questions

  • How have courts resolved (or dodged) the conflict between border control powers and protection obligations in your chosen doctrine?
  • Where do domestic rulings and international obligations (1951 Convention, ECHR, ICCPR) diverge?
  • What is the trajectory of the doctrine, and what would principled reform look like?

Methods

Doctrinal legal analysis of 4–6 key cases plus treaty texts and scholarly commentary. Non-law students may use case summaries and secondary analyses with instructor guidance.

IRB / research ethics

Not required — public legal documents.

Deliverables

  • 4,500-word legal analysis memo with a case-line map
  • 10-minute moot presentation defending one side of the tension, then critiquing your own argument

Grading criteria

  • Accuracy of legal analysis (35%)
  • Handling of the domestic/international interface (25%)
  • Argument and counterargument quality (25%)
  • Citation and clarity (15%)

  • DOIExternal Border Control Techniques in the EU as a Challenge to the Principle of Non-Refoulement
    Nagy, Boldizsár & Goldner Lang, Iris · 2021 · European Constitutional Law Review, 17(3), 476–509 · 10.1017/S1574019621000249

    Systematically analyzes how EU border practices—pushbacks, 'safe country' designations, deterrence policies—undermine non-refoulement under EU and international law. Essential for teaching the tension between state sovereignty, domestic law, and international refugee law obligations in a comparative EU context.

  • DOINon-Refoulement in a World of Cooperative Deterrence
    Hathaway, James C. & Gammeltoft-Hansen, Thomas · 2015 · Columbia Journal of Transnational Law, 53(2), 235–284 · 10.2139/SSRN.2479511

    Hathaway and Gammeltoft-Hansen argue that cooperative interdiction schemes between states allow systematic non-refoulement violations by diffusing legal responsibility. A landmark article in international refugee law that illustrates how states use inter-state cooperation to evade obligations, fundamental for teaching deportation and detention policy.

    Access article →
  • DOIThe Emerging Migration State
    Hollifield, James F. · 2004 · International Migration Review, 38(3), 885–912 · 10.1111/j.1747-7379.2004.tb00223.x

    Hollifield introduces the concept of the 'liberal paradox'—the tension between states' desires to control immigration and the rights-based constraints that liberal democracies impose on themselves. A foundational piece for teaching the interaction between sovereignty, domestic law, and international norms in migration governance.

    Access article →
  • DOIA European Narrative of Border Externalisation: The European Trust Fund for Africa Story
    Pacciardi, Agnese · 2024 · European Security, 33(1), 91–110 · 10.1080/09662839.2024.2304723

    Critically examines the EU Trust Fund for Africa as a case study in how development aid is weaponized as a migration-control tool, blurring the line between humanitarian and security objectives. Provides concrete policy material for teaching the 'migration-development nexus' and border externalization strategies.

    Access article →
  • DOIUnderstanding the Crisis of Refugee Law: Legal Scholarship and the EU Asylum System
    Noll, Gregor; Vedsted-Hansen, Jens; & Byrne, Rosemary · 2020 · Leiden Journal of International Law, 33(4), 871–891 · 10.1017/S0922156520000382

    Examines the widening gap between international refugee law ideals and the political-institutional realities of EU border governance. Useful for a comparative policy analysis of how sovereignty pressures have progressively restricted asylum access through legal mechanisms rather than overt refusal.

    Access article →
  • DOIContainment Instead of Refoulement: Shifting State Responsibility in the Age of Cooperative Migration Control
    Pijnenburg, Annick · 2020 · Human Rights Law Review, 20(2), 306–334 · 10.2139/ssrn.3418365

    Argues that destination states are increasingly using transit states as proxies to contain migrants, shifting—and often evading—responsibility for non-refoulement violations. Directly addresses the contemporary form of border enforcement through cooperative deterrence and complicity under international law.
