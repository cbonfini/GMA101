1 Module overview

Introduces key concepts, terminology, and theoretical frameworks used to understand migration, displacement, refugees, and mobility. Establishes migration as a global system and engages the tension between the right to migrate and the right not to migrate, separating myths from realities.

Skill emphasis: Conceptual + Reflective

◎ Learning outcomes

Undergraduate

  1. Define key migration terms
  2. Distinguish voluntary and forced migration types
  3. Explain why migration is a global and historical phenomenon
  4. Reflect on personal assumptions and narratives about migration

Graduate

  1. Critically evaluate major migration frameworks and theories
  2. Synthesize competing definitions across disciplines
  3. Apply theory to contemporary migration cases

✦ Signature learning experience

✎

Active-learning assignment

Reflective essay: “What shapes migration narratives?”

View full assignment sheet✎

Purpose

This reflective essay anchors the whole course: before studying data, law, or policy, students examine where their own ideas about migration came from. The essay asks students to trace the sources of their assumptions — family stories, media, politics, religion, personal encounters — and test them against the module's concepts and evidence.

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Define key migration terms accurately in your own writing (UG 1)
  • Distinguish migration types when describing real or remembered examples (UG 2)
  • Explain global migration patterns that confirm or contradict your assumptions (UG 3)
  • Reflect on personal assumptions and where they originated (UG 4)

Graduate extension

Graduate students add a theoretical layer: identify which migration framework (e.g., push–pull, aspiration/ability, world systems) their inherited narrative implicitly assumed, and critique that framework using at least three peer-reviewed sources (Grad 1–2).

Assignment steps

  1. Before doing the readings, free-write for 20 minutes: What do I believe about who migrates, why, and whether it is good or bad? Where did each belief come from? Keep this un-edited draft.
  2. Complete the module readings and lecture; note every point where the evidence surprised you or contradicted your free-write.
  3. Choose 2–3 of your strongest initial assumptions and research each against course sources (de Haas, Carling, UN/IOM data).
  4. Write the essay: describe the narrative you held, its sources, what the evidence says, and how your understanding has shifted (or been confirmed — honest confirmation is acceptable if evidenced).
  5. Use at least five key terms from the module correctly (migrant, refugee, asylum seeker, IDP, forced/voluntary migration, involuntary immobility).
  6. Close with one question about migration you now want the rest of the course to answer.

Deliverables & format

  • Essay, 1,200–1,500 words (graduate: 2,000–2,500 with theory critique)
  • Original free-write attached as an appendix (ungraded, required)
  • Minimum 4 course sources cited (graduate: 7, at least 3 peer-reviewed)

Grading criteria

  • Accurate use of migration terminology (25%)
  • Quality of evidence used to test assumptions (30%)
  • Depth and honesty of reflection — genuine examination, not performance (30%)
  • Writing clarity and citation (15%)

⚑ Service learning project

⚑

Community Story Booth: Listening Across Migration Narratives

Partner with a local refugee resettlement agency or immigrant community center to co-host a community story-sharing event, then examine how the narratives you heard confirm or challenge common migration myths.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Define key migration terms in real-world context (UG 1)
  • Distinguish migration types encountered in community narratives (UG 2)
  • Reflect on personal assumptions about migration (UG 4)

Suggested community partners

  • Refugee resettlement agencies (e.g., Catholic Charities, IRC, HIAS affiliates)
  • Immigrant community or cultural centers
  • Campus international student services or ESL conversation programs
  • Public libraries with newcomer programming

Project steps

  1. Complete the partner's volunteer orientation and the course's ethical-engagement briefing (consent, confidentiality, no pressure to share).
  2. Before the event, write a 1-page pre-reflection documenting your current assumptions about who migrates and why.
  3. Help the partner plan and host a story-sharing or conversation-circle event (or join an existing program for 3–4 sessions).
  4. Keep a listening journal: record themes, terminology used by community members, and moments that surprised you (no identifying details).
  5. Write a post-reflection comparing your pre-reflection with what you heard, using correct course terminology (migrant, refugee, asylum seeker, IDP).
  6. Debrief with the partner and share a thank-you summary of what students learned.

Deliverables

  • Pre- and post-reflection essays (2–3 pages total)
  • Listening journal (de-identified)
  • One-page 'myths vs. realities' insight sheet suitable for the partner to reuse

Grading criteria

  • Preparation and professionalism with the partner (20%)
  • Correct, careful use of migration terminology (25%)
  • Depth of reflection on assumptions and narratives (35%)
  • Quality and usefulness of the insight sheet (20%)

Time commitment: 12–15 hours across the module (orientation, event/sessions, reflection). Ethics: Community members are never asked to recount trauma. Stories are shared voluntarily; nothing identifying leaves the room without written consent.

✍ Graduate research project

✍

Competing Frameworks: A Critical Synthesis of Migration Theory

A structured literature review that critically evaluates the major theoretical frameworks of migration and tests them against one contemporary migration flow of your choice.

View full assignment sheet✎

Aligned learning outcomes

  • Critically evaluate migration frameworks (Grad 1)
  • Synthesize competing definitions across disciplines (Grad 2)

Sample research questions

  • Which framework best explains a chosen contemporary flow (e.g., Venezuela–Colombia, West Africa–Canary Islands), and where does it fail?
  • How do definitional choices (migrant vs. refugee vs. IDP) change what each theory can see?
  • What does Carling's aspiration/ability model add that classic push–pull cannot capture?

Methods

Systematic reading of 10–12 peer-reviewed sources (start from the module reading list: Massey et al. 1993; de Haas 2010; Carling 2002, 2024), followed by a single-case application using published data (UN DESA, IOM, UNHCR). No human subjects.

IRB / research ethics

Not required — secondary literature and published data only.

Deliverables

  • 4,000–5,000-word critical review with a one-page visual 'theory map'
  • 15-minute seminar presentation defending your framework assessment

Grading criteria

  • Accuracy and coverage of the theoretical landscape (30%)
  • Quality of critical evaluation, not just summary (30%)
  • Rigor of the case application (25%)
  • Writing, citation, and presentation (15%)

▤ Peer-reviewed readings

Show 6 readings6
  • DOITheories of International Migration: A Review and Appraisal
    Massey, D. S., Arango, J., Hugo, G., Kouaouci, A., Pellegrino, A., & Taylor, J. E. · 1993 · Population and Development Review, 19(3), 431–466 · 10.2307/2938462

    The foundational canonical review article that systematically evaluates neoclassical economics, new economics of labor migration, dual labor market theory, world systems theory, network theory, and institutional theory. Indispensable as a starting point for any migration theory unit because it maps the entire theoretical landscape in one place.

    Access article →
  • DOIMigration and Development: A Theoretical Perspective
    de Haas, Hein · 2010 · International Migration Review, 44(1), 227–264 · 10.1111/j.1747-7379.2009.00804.x

    De Haas synthesizes optimistic and pessimistic views of migration's development impact by integrating the new economics of labor migration, livelihood approaches, and transnational perspectives. A key theoretical piece that shows why migration outcomes are heterogeneous and context-dependent, and demonstrates how paradigm shifts in social theory shape migration debates.

    Access article →
  • DOIMigration in the Age of Involuntary Immobility: Theoretical Reflections and Cape Verdean Experiences
    Carling, Jørgen · 2002 · Journal of Ethnic and Migration Studies, 28(1), 5–42 · 10.1080/13691830120103912

    Carling introduces the aspiration/ability framework, distinguishing between migrants' desire to move and their capacity to do so. This article is essential for teaching the voluntary–forced migration continuum and the structural constraints that produce 'involuntary immobility', a concept now central to migration theory.

    Access article →
  • DOIWho is a Migrant? Abandoning the Nation-state Point of View in the Study of Migration
    Tazzioli, Martina & Scheel, Stephan · 2022 · Migration Politics, 1(1), 002 · 10.21468/migpol.1.1.002

    Critically examines how nation-state-centred definitions of 'migrant' embed methodological nationalism and create stigmatizing labels. Faculty teaching definitional debates (refugee vs. migrant vs. IDP) will find this article provocative and useful for introducing students to the politics of categorization.

    Access article →
  • DOIWhy Do People Migrate? Fresh Takes on the Foundational Question of Migration Studies
    Carling, Jørgen · 2024 · International Migration Review, 58(4), 1639–1662 · 10.1177/01979183241269445

    Carling revisits migration theory's central question from multiple angles including push–pull, capabilities, volition, and opportunity frameworks. This recent article provides an accessible yet theoretically rigorous entry point that debunks simplistic explanations while introducing students to multi-causal thinking.

    Access article →
  • DOIMigration Policies Are Problematic – Because They Are About Migration
    Castles, Stephen · 2017 · Ethnic and Racial Studies, 40(9), 1538–1542 · 10.1080/01419870.2017.1308532

    Castles argues that migration policies fail because states try to manage what are inherently social transformation processes, not controllable flows. This piece is ideal for introducing the 'myths vs. realities' theme and for contextualizing why restrictive policies rarely achieve their stated goals.

    Access article →
